# Access to Fair Financial Assets and Affordable Financing: Savings, Credit, and Inflation Resilience

**Short description:** 

For billions of people the hard problem is not earning money but keeping it and putting it to work. Savings lose value to inflation within weeks, and formal accounts, where they exist, demand minimums and paperwork that exclude low-income savers while paying interest below inflation. Financing is harder still. Small business owners and informal workers borrow at 30 to 100 percent or more, or go without entirely, even though they hold real productive value in a motorbike, a stored harvest, shop inventory, or an invoice from a reliable buyer. They are rarely poor credit risks. Lenders simply cannot cheaply verify what a borrower owns, establish a claim on it, or recover it after default, so underwriting a small loan costs more than it earns. Tokenization is dismantling both barriers, with stablecoins hedging local currency volatility and tokenized money market funds accepting minimums measured in tens of dollars, but almost none of that machinery reaches ordinary savers or the assets they actually hold. This space covers both sides of a household or small-business balance sheet: what people can safely hold and grow, and what they can borrow against it.

**Longer description**

**Looking at:**

1. Everyday stores of value: dollar-denominated stablecoins in high-inflation economies, tokenized money market funds, stablebonds, commodities, fractionalized property
2. Micro-savings and micro-investing with very low minimums; community savings models (ROSCAs, chamas, tandas); payroll-linked and goal-based saving
3. Credit for productive use and for household resilience: working capital, asset and equipment finance, inventory and trade credit, emergency liquidity, school fees, refinancing of high-cost debt
4. Collateral and cash flows people actually hold: vehicles, equipment, inventory, stored crops, receivables, savings balances, recurring income streams
5. Loan structures that fit irregular and seasonal income; legal enforceability across jurisdictions

**Challenges and Focus:**

1. Inflation and currency volatility erode savings faster than any accessible formal account can compensate
2. Minimums, fees, and paperwork exclude low-income savers, pushing them into cash, livestock, or goods that are illiquid, risky, and excluded from growth
3. Institutional-grade tokenized assets now exist, but nothing translates them into products everyday people can actually use
4. Underwriting and servicing costs exceed what a small loan can earn, so the borrower is either refused or priced punitively
5. Rigid repayment schedules ignore seasonal and volatile income, turning temporary liquidity constraints into defaults
6. Predatory debt structures, hidden fees, and severe collateral risks can compromise a borrower's livelihood and lead to debt spirals
7. Women are disproportionately excluded on both sides. They save heavily through informal channels but are least served formally, and they often lack formal title to the assets they use daily

**Key Challenges Facing This Category:**

1. Regulatory treatment of tokenized securities and stablecoins varies by market, as does legal recognition of digital claims on physical assets
2. Valuation data for informal assets is thin, and liquidation may be impractical or ethically unacceptable
3. Cheaper capital does not automatically become cheaper credit, so the saving must reach the borrower
4. Yield must be balanced against consumer protection, and products must not become speculation vehicles for vulnerable users
5. Off-ramp availability when savers need local currency, and trust in unfamiliar assets and custody arrangements
6. Fair pricing and commercial viability can pull in opposite directions at very small ticket sizes
7. Earning trust from people previously harmed by predatory lenders or by currency collapse

**Guidelines for solutions:**

*Make saving and lending structurally cheaper rather than offering subsidized rates, and be specific about what "fair" means in your product: the all-in cost, the repayment structure, the real yield net of fees, and what happens when a borrower falls behind. Name the revenue model (spread, origination, servicing, asset-management or subscription fees, distribution partnerships with asset issuers, or infrastructure sold to lenders) and a specific customer segment. Be explicit about custody: who holds the asset, and what happens if the provider disappears. Use Stellar's asset issuance, tokenized-asset ecosystem, stablecoin liquidity, and Soroban contracts for tokenization, escrow, and programmable repayment.*

***SDG alignment: SDGs 1, 2 (smallholder finance), 4 (embedded financial literacy), 5, 8, 9, 10***
