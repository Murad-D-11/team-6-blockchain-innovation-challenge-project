# Verification Infrastructure for the Next Billion Customers

**Short description:** 

Roughly one billion people cannot prove who they are to any financial institution's satisfaction, and hundreds of millions more have documents but no financial history. Institutions are blocked by cost rather than indifference. Onboarding a single emerging-market user runs five to thirty dollars, legitimate applicants are rejected by rules built for a different population, and every provider repeats the same verification work. The problem now extends to autonomous software agents transacting on behalf of people, whose identity and authorization must also be proven. 

This space asks how identity, eligibility, and trustworthiness can be established once and reused across providers and borders without turning the customer into a surveillance subject: verification without traditional documents, alternative credit signals, proofs that reveal a conclusion but not the underlying data, and recovery after losing both phone and papers. Every team should answer one question: who does the solution exclude?

**Longer description:** 

**Looking at:**

1. Verification for people lacking conventional documents, addresses, or credit files
2. Portable, reusable credentials that travel across providers and borders
3. Alternative signals of identity, income, and reliability drawn from payment, mobile, utility, and commerce history
4. Proving a single fact (age, residency, income band, sanctions status) without disclosing the underlying data
5. Recovery after losing both device and documents, and delegated authority within households

**Challenges and Focus:**

1. Onboarding an emerging-market user costs five to thirty dollars, which makes desirable customers unprofitable before they ever transact
2. Rejection rules built for a different population exclude legitimate applicants, and the institution never learns it was wrong
3. Verification work is endlessly duplicated, since every provider re-verifies the same person from scratch
4. KYC/AML obligations are non-negotiable, so anonymity is not an answer and the goal is proportionate disclosure rather than none
5. Verification systems can become instruments of surveillance or exclusion, and the harm lands on people with the least recourse
6. Women disproportionately lack the documents, devices, and address histories these systems assume
7. A credential has little value until many institutions accept it, and institutions have little reason to accept one that few others already use

**Key Challenges Facing This Category:**

1. Institutions must accept credentials they did not issue, which is a commercial and cultural problem as much as a technical one
2. Regulatory acceptance moves slowly, and supervisors differ market by market on what satisfies an obligation
3. Any exclusion built into the system is inherited by every adopter, at scale
4. Breach costs fall on the most vulnerable, who can least absorb identity compromise
5. Value depends on network acceptance that does not exist at launch
6. Biometrics raise consent, revocation, and permanence problems that documents do not

**Guidelines for solutions:**

Name the paying customer, usually the institution that wants the end user. Be explicit about who the solution excludes and how that is mitigated, and show how the credential gains value as acceptance grows, including how you win the first ten institutions before the network exists. Prove the unit economics: what does verification cost per user at your scale, against the five to thirty dollars it replaces? Credentials and authorization logic can anchor on Stellar, and anchors, wallets, ramps, remittance providers, and the disbursing institutions in Space 3 are a natural first customer base.

***SDG alignment: SDGs 5, 9, 10; identity outcomes supporting SDG 1***
